## Diversification: Do Not Put All Eggs in One Basket
Diversification means spreading investments across many assets that do not all move together, so a problem in one does not sink the whole portfolio. It reduces risk without necessarily reducing expected return. Diversify across asset classes (stocks, bonds), geography (U.S. and international), sectors and company sizes. A single broad index fund already holds hundreds or thousands of companies.

## Asset Allocation
Asset allocation is how you divide your portfolio among stocks, bonds, cash and other assets. Research suggests it explains most of the variation in a portfolio's long-term behaviour. A younger investor saving for retirement in 30 years might hold 80-90% stocks, while someone five years from a goal might hold 40-50%. Your allocation should reflect your goals, time horizon and ability to tolerate losses.

## Rebalancing
Over time, strong-performing assets grow into a larger share of your portfolio and shift your risk level. Rebalancing means selling some of what has grown and buying what has lagged to return to your target mix. Common approaches are rebalancing once a year or when an asset class drifts more than 5 percentage points from its target. In taxable accounts, consider using new contributions to rebalance to avoid taxes.

## Dollar-Cost Averaging
Dollar-cost averaging means investing a fixed amount at regular intervals regardless of price, such as $300 every month. You buy more shares when prices are low and fewer when high. It reduces the risk of investing everything right before a drop and builds a habit. Studies show investing a lump sum immediately often does slightly better on average, but averaging in can feel safer.

## Concentration Risk
Concentration risk arises when a large share of your wealth is in one stock, sector or asset. Employer stock is a common trap, because your job and your savings depend on the same company. A frequently cited guideline is to keep any single stock at 5-10% or less of your portfolio unless you accept the added risk.

## Home Bias and International Diversification
Many investors hold mostly domestic stocks, called home bias. Different countries' markets lead in different periods, so adding international stocks can reduce risk and broaden opportunity. A common beginner mix includes 20-40% of the stock portion in international funds.

## The Three-Fund Portfolio
A popular simple portfolio from the Bogleheads community uses three low-cost index funds: a total U.S. stock market fund, a total international stock fund and a total bond market fund. Adjusting the percentage in each controls risk. It offers broad diversification, low costs and minimal maintenance.

## Measuring Portfolio Performance
Total return includes price change plus dividends and interest. Compare your performance with an appropriate benchmark, such as the S&P 500 for a U.S. large-cap stock portfolio, not with unrelated indexes. Be cautious with short periods; results over one year say little about the quality of a strategy.
