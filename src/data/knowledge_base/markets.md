## Stock Market Indexes
An index tracks the performance of a group of securities. The S&P 500 follows 500 large U.S. companies, the Dow Jones Industrial Average follows 30 large companies and is price-weighted, the Nasdaq-100 is dominated by technology and growth companies, and the Russell 2000 tracks small caps. You cannot invest in an index directly but can buy funds that track one.

## Bull and Bear Markets
A bull market is a sustained rise of 20% or more from a low; a bear market is a decline of 20% or more from a high. Historically, bull markets have lasted longer and gained more than bear markets have lost, but timing the turns is extremely difficult. Staying invested through both has historically served long-term investors better than trying to jump in and out.

## Interest Rates and the Stock Market
When the Federal Reserve raises interest rates, borrowing costs rise and bonds become more attractive, which can pressure stock valuations, particularly for growth companies. When rates fall, the opposite often occurs. The reaction depends on whether changes were expected, so markets often move on surprises or on changes in the outlook.

## Moving Averages and Trends
A moving average smooths prices over a period such as 20 or 50 days. A price above its 50-day average suggests an uptrend; below suggests a downtrend. When a short-term average crosses above a longer one, traders call it a golden cross and a cross below a death cross. These indicators describe the past and are not reliable predictors on their own.

## The VIX Volatility Index
The VIX measures expected 30-day volatility in the S&P 500 from options prices and is often called the fear gauge. Readings below about 15 suggest calm markets, readings above 30 indicate high stress. A high VIX signals uncertainty, not necessarily a falling market, and long-term investors generally should not trade on it.

## Inflation, GDP and Economic Indicators
Markets react to indicators such as inflation (CPI), gross domestic product (GDP), unemployment and consumer sentiment. Strong growth with moderate inflation is usually positive for stocks; high inflation can lead the Fed to raise rates. No single data point determines market direction, so view them as context rather than trading signals.

## Market Orders and Limit Orders
A market order buys or sells immediately at the best available price; a limit order sets the maximum price you will pay or the minimum you will accept and executes only if the market reaches it. Limit orders give price control, which matters for thinly traded securities. A stop order becomes a market order when a trigger price is hit.

## Market Liquidity and Trading Hours
Regular U.S. stock market hours are 9:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. Eastern, Monday to Friday, excluding holidays. Liquidity describes how easily an asset can be traded without moving its price; large-cap stocks and major ETFs are highly liquid, while small or obscure securities may have wide bid-ask spreads that increase your cost.
