## Starting Retirement Saving Early
Because of compounding, the first dollars you invest have the longest to grow. For example, investing $300 a month from age 25 to 65 at a 6.5% annual return grows to roughly $0.7 million, while starting at 35 produces about $0.35 million. Even small amounts started early can outweigh larger amounts started late.

## How Much Do I Need to Retire
A common rule of thumb is to save 25 times your expected annual spending, based on the 4% rule: withdrawing 4% of the starting portfolio in the first year, then adjusting for inflation, has historically supported about 30 years of retirement. If you expect to spend $50,000 a year beyond Social Security, a target near $1.25 million is a rough starting point. Your own number depends on spending, longevity, taxes and other income.

## The 4% Rule and Safe Withdrawal Rates
The 4% rule comes from historical U.S. studies of 30-year retirements. It suggests withdrawing 4% of your initial portfolio in year one and adjusting that dollar amount for inflation each year. It is a guideline, not a guarantee: longer retirements, lower expected returns or poor early market performance may call for a lower rate, such as 3.5%.

## Social Security Basics
Social Security provides a retirement benefit based on your 35 highest-earning years. You can claim as early as 62 at a permanently reduced amount, at full retirement age (66-67 depending on birth year) for the full benefit, or as late as 70 for a larger benefit. Delaying generally increases the monthly payment. Consider it one income source, not your entire plan.

## Employer Match: Free Money
Many employers match a portion of your 401(k) contributions, for example 50 cents per dollar up to 6% of salary. The match is an immediate return on your contribution, so contributing at least enough to receive the full match is usually the first priority. Check your plan's vesting schedule, which determines when matched money becomes yours.

## Retirement Savings Rates
Many planners suggest saving 10-15% of pre-tax income for retirement over a career, including any employer match. If you start late, you may need more. Increasing your contribution by 1% each year or with each raise is an easy way to build the habit without feeling a pinch.
