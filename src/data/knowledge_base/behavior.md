## Common Behavioural Biases
Investors are human and make predictable mistakes: loss aversion (feeling losses about twice as strongly as gains), overconfidence, herd behaviour, recency bias (assuming recent trends will continue) and confirmation bias. Naming these biases helps you set rules in advance, such as a written plan, automatic contributions and scheduled rebalancing, so emotions have less influence.

## Market Timing Rarely Works
Missing just a handful of the market's best days can dramatically lower long-term returns, and those best days often occur close to the worst ones. Few professionals consistently time the market, so most beginners do better by investing regularly and staying invested. Time in the market generally beats timing the market.

## Avoiding Scams and Hype
Warning signs of investment fraud include guaranteed high returns, pressure to act fast, unsolicited offers, secret strategies and difficulty withdrawing money. Verify advisors and brokers through the SEC's Investor.gov and FINRA BrokerCheck, and be wary of social-media tips, "pump and dump" schemes and unregulated crypto offerings. If it sounds too good to be true, it probably is.

## Investing in Meme Stocks and Speculation
Speculative trades driven by social media hype can produce sharp gains and equally sharp losses. If you want to speculate, many educators suggest limiting it to a small share of your portfolio, say 5% or less, with money you can afford to lose, and keeping the core of your portfolio in diversified funds.

## Fiduciary vs Non-Fiduciary Advisors
A fiduciary is legally obligated to act in your best interest. Not all financial professionals are fiduciaries; some may earn commissions on products they sell. Ask whether an advisor is a fiduciary, how they are paid and what the total fees are. Fee-only advisors are paid directly by clients rather than by commissions.
