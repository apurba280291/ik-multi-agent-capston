## Understanding Investment Risk
Investment risk is the chance that returns differ from what you expect, including losing money. It comes in many forms: market risk (the whole market falls), inflation risk, interest-rate risk, credit risk, liquidity risk and concentration risk. Not investing is also risky, because inflation erodes cash. The goal is to take the right amount of risk for your goals, not to eliminate it.

## Risk Tolerance vs Risk Capacity
Risk tolerance is how much volatility you can emotionally handle; risk capacity is how much loss your finances can absorb. A young investor with stable income has high capacity, but may have low tolerance if market drops cause panic selling. A retiree might have high tolerance but low capacity because they depend on the portfolio for income. A good plan respects the lower of the two.

## Volatility and Standard Deviation
Volatility measures how much an investment's returns vary. It is usually expressed as the standard deviation of returns: a fund with a 15% standard deviation has typically moved within about 15 percentage points of its average in roughly two out of three years. Stocks are more volatile than bonds. Higher volatility means a bumpier ride, not necessarily a worse investment.

## Drawdowns and Bear Markets
A drawdown is the decline from a portfolio's peak to a later low. A bear market is a drop of 20% or more in a broad market index; the U.S. stock market has experienced several, including declines of over 30% in 2008 and 2020, followed by recoveries. Recoveries can take months or years. Knowing the possible drawdown of your allocation before you invest helps you avoid selling at the bottom.

## Sequence of Returns Risk
Sequence-of-returns risk is the danger that poor market returns early in retirement, when you begin withdrawals, permanently damage a portfolio even if long-run average returns are fine. Selling investments after a crash locks in losses. Holding a cash or bond buffer and keeping withdrawal rates modest, around 4% of the initial balance a common planning rule of thumb, reduces this risk.

## Beta and Correlation
Beta measures how much an asset moves relative to the market: a beta of 1.2 suggests it tends to move 20% more than the market. Correlation measures how similar two assets' movements are on a scale from -1 to 1. Combining assets with low correlation, such as stocks and high-quality bonds, lowers overall portfolio volatility.

## Sharpe Ratio
The Sharpe ratio measures return earned per unit of risk: (portfolio return - risk-free rate) divided by volatility. A higher Sharpe ratio means better risk-adjusted performance. Values around 1 are considered good and above 2 are rare over long periods. It treats upside and downside volatility alike, so use it as one tool among several.
