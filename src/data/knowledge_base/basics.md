## Why Invest Instead of Only Saving
Saving keeps money safe and available; investing aims to grow it faster than inflation over the long run. Cash in a savings account usually earns a modest rate, and rising prices slowly reduce what that cash can buy. Investments such as stocks and bonds carry the risk of loss, but historically diversified portfolios have outpaced inflation over long periods. A common approach is to save for short-term needs (under about five years) and invest money you will not need for longer.

## Compound Interest Explained
Compound interest means you earn returns on your original money and on the returns already earned. If you invest $1,000 at 7% a year, you have $1,070 after year one, and in year two you earn 7% on $1,070, not on $1,000. The effect is small early and large later, which is why starting early matters so much. The Rule of 72 gives a quick estimate: divide 72 by the annual return to estimate the years needed to double your money (72 / 7 is about 10 years).

## The Rule of 72
The Rule of 72 is a mental shortcut for estimating how long it takes money to double. Divide 72 by the annual rate of return: at 4% it takes about 18 years, at 6% about 12 years and at 9% about 8 years. It also works for inflation: at 3% inflation, prices double in roughly 24 years. It is an approximation and works best for rates between 5% and 10%.

## Inflation and Purchasing Power
Inflation is the general rise in prices over time. If inflation is 3% a year, something that costs $100 today costs about $134 in ten years. This is why the real return of an investment matters: real return is roughly the nominal return minus inflation. A savings account paying 2% during 3% inflation loses purchasing power even though the balance grows.

## Building an Emergency Fund First
Before investing, most educators suggest an emergency fund covering three to six months of essential expenses, held in a safe, accessible account such as a high-yield savings account. The fund prevents you from selling investments at a bad time to cover a surprise bill or job loss. People with variable income or dependents often aim for the higher end of the range.

## Risk and Return Trade-off
Higher potential returns come with higher risk of loss and larger price swings. Cash and short-term government bonds sit at the low-risk end; stocks are higher; concentrated or speculative assets are higher still. There is no reliable way to earn high returns without taking on risk, so be skeptical of any promise of high returns with no risk. Your risk tolerance depends on your time horizon, financial stability and how you react to losses.

## Time Value of Money
A dollar today is worth more than a dollar in the future because it can be invested and earn returns. Present value discounts future money back to today's terms; future value projects today's money forward. This idea underlies retirement planning, loan payments and the valuation of bonds and stocks.
