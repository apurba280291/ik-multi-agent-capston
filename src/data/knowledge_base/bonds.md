## What Is a Bond
A bond is a loan you make to a government or company. In return the issuer pays you interest (the coupon) and repays the face value (principal) on the maturity date. Bonds are generally less volatile than stocks and provide income, which is why they are used to stabilise portfolios. They still carry risks: the issuer could default and prices fall when interest rates rise.

## Bond Prices and Interest Rates
Bond prices and interest rates move in opposite directions. If new bonds pay higher rates, existing lower-rate bonds become less attractive and their prices fall. Longer-maturity bonds react more strongly, a sensitivity measured by duration: a bond fund with a duration of 6 years would be expected to fall roughly 6% if rates rose 1 percentage point. If you hold an individual bond to maturity, you still receive the full principal unless the issuer defaults.

## Treasury Bills, Notes and Bonds
U.S. Treasury securities are backed by the U.S. government and are considered among the safest investments in terms of default risk. Treasury bills mature in a year or less, notes in 2-10 years and bonds in 20-30 years. Interest is subject to federal income tax but exempt from state and local income tax.

## Corporate Bonds and Credit Ratings
Corporate bonds pay more than Treasuries because companies are more likely to default. Rating agencies grade credit quality: bonds rated BBB- or higher are investment grade; those below are high-yield ("junk") bonds that pay more but carry greater default risk. Beginners typically start with investment-grade or diversified bond funds.

## Municipal Bonds
Municipal bonds are issued by states and cities. Interest is generally exempt from federal income tax and sometimes from state tax, which makes them attractive to investors in higher tax brackets in taxable accounts. They are usually not useful inside tax-advantaged accounts such as IRAs, where the tax exemption adds no benefit.

## Yield, Coupon and Yield to Maturity
The coupon is the fixed interest a bond pays as a percentage of face value. The current yield is annual coupon divided by current price. Yield to maturity (YTM) estimates the total annual return if you hold the bond until maturity, including price change. Yield and price move in opposite directions.

## Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS)
Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities adjust their principal with inflation, so the interest payments and the final value rise when consumer prices rise. They help protect purchasing power but can still lose value in the short term when real interest rates rise.
