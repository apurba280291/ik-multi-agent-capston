## What Is an ETF
An exchange-traded fund (ETF) holds a basket of investments, such as hundreds of stocks or bonds, and trades on an exchange like a single stock. One purchase gives you instant diversification at low cost. ETFs can track an index (for example the S&P 500 via SPY or VOO), a sector, a bond market or a commodity. Check the fund's expense ratio, holdings and what index it follows.

## What Is a Mutual Fund
A mutual fund pools money from many investors to buy a diversified portfolio managed by a professional or tracking an index. Unlike ETFs, mutual funds are priced once per day after the market closes and often have minimum initial investments. Some charge sales loads or high fees, so compare costs carefully.

## ETF vs Mutual Fund
Both offer diversification. ETFs trade throughout the day, usually have no minimum beyond the price of one share, and are often more tax-efficient in taxable accounts. Mutual funds trade once a day, allow automatic investment of exact dollar amounts and can be convenient in workplace plans. For comparable index exposure, the lower expense ratio is generally the better choice.

## Index Funds and Passive Investing
An index fund tries to match a market index instead of beating it. Because there is little trading and no expensive research, costs are low. Decades of data show most actively managed funds fail to outperform their benchmark after fees over long periods, which is the main reason many investors, including the Bogleheads community, favour broad low-cost index funds.

## Expense Ratios and Fees
The expense ratio is the annual fee a fund charges as a percentage of your investment. A 0.03% fee costs $3 per year on $10,000; a 1% fee costs $100. Fees compound against you: on a 30-year investment, a 1% higher fee can reduce the ending balance by roughly a quarter. Compare expense ratios among funds with similar holdings.

## Target-Date Funds
A target-date fund picks an asset mix based on a retirement year (for example "2055") and gradually becomes more conservative as that year approaches. It is a one-fund, hands-off solution popular in 401(k) plans. Check the expense ratio and the fund's glide path, since both vary between providers.

## Bond Funds vs Individual Bonds
A bond fund holds many bonds and has no maturity date, so its price keeps fluctuating with interest rates. An individual bond returns its principal at maturity. Funds offer diversification and easy reinvestment; individual bonds offer a known maturity value if the issuer does not default.
