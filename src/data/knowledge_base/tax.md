## 401(k) Plans
A 401(k) is an employer-sponsored retirement plan. Traditional 401(k) contributions are made with pre-tax dollars, lowering your taxable income now, and withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income. Many plans also offer a Roth 401(k), funded with after-tax dollars and tax-free qualified withdrawals. For 2025 the employee contribution limit was $23,500 (plus catch-up contributions for those 50 and older). Limits are adjusted every year, so confirm the current figure at IRS.gov. Withdrawals before age 59 and a half generally face a 10% penalty plus tax, with some exceptions.

## Traditional IRA
A traditional Individual Retirement Account lets you contribute money that may be tax-deductible, depending on your income and whether you have a workplace plan. Investments grow tax-deferred and withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income. Required minimum distributions begin at age 73 for most people. For 2025 the IRA contribution limit was $7,000 ($8,000 if 50 or older); verify current limits at IRS.gov.

## Roth IRA
A Roth IRA is funded with after-tax dollars, grows tax-free and allows tax-free qualified withdrawals after age 59 and a half if the account has been open at least five years. You can withdraw your contributions (not earnings) at any time without tax or penalty. Eligibility to contribute phases out at higher incomes. Roth accounts are particularly attractive if you expect to be in a higher tax bracket in retirement.

## Traditional vs Roth: Which Is Better
The key question is whether your tax rate is higher now or in retirement. If you expect a lower rate in retirement, the up-front deduction of a traditional account may win; if you expect a higher rate, paying tax now with a Roth may win. Many people split contributions between both to diversify their future tax exposure. Roth accounts also have no required minimum distributions for the original owner.

## Health Savings Account (HSA)
An HSA is available with a high-deductible health plan and offers a triple tax advantage: contributions are tax-deductible, growth is tax-free and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free. After 65, withdrawals for non-medical purposes are taxed as income without penalty. Many people invest the HSA balance for the long term and pay current medical costs out of pocket.

## 529 College Savings Plan
A 529 plan is a tax-advantaged account for education savings. Contributions are made with after-tax dollars (some states offer a deduction), growth is tax-free and withdrawals for qualified education expenses are tax-free. Qualified costs include tuition, fees, books and room and board, and up to a limit, K-12 tuition and student loan repayments. If the beneficiary does not use the funds, you can change beneficiaries.

## Capital Gains Tax
When you sell an investment for more than you paid, the profit is a capital gain. Assets held one year or less produce short-term gains taxed as ordinary income; assets held longer than one year produce long-term gains taxed at lower rates (0%, 15% or 20% for most investors, depending on income). Gains inside retirement accounts are not taxed until withdrawal, or never for Roth accounts. Losses can offset gains.

## Tax-Loss Harvesting and the Wash-Sale Rule
Tax-loss harvesting means selling an investment at a loss to offset capital gains and up to $3,000 of ordinary income per year, then reinvesting the proceeds. The wash-sale rule disallows the loss if you buy the same or a substantially identical security within 30 days before or after the sale. It applies only in taxable accounts.

## Tax Brackets and Marginal Rates
The U.S. uses progressive tax brackets: each rate applies only to the income within that bracket, not to all your income. Moving into a higher bracket does not make all your earnings taxed at the higher rate. Your marginal rate is the rate on your last dollar of income and is the relevant one when deciding whether a deduction or contribution saves tax.

## Tax-Efficient Fund Placement
Asset location means putting investments in the accounts where they are taxed least. Tax-inefficient assets, such as bonds and REITs that generate ordinary income, often belong in tax-deferred accounts; tax-efficient index funds fit well in taxable accounts; and the highest-growth assets can be attractive in Roth accounts.

## Standard Deduction and Itemizing
The standard deduction is a fixed amount that reduces taxable income without needing receipts. You can instead itemize deductions, such as mortgage interest, state and local taxes and charitable gifts, if they total more than the standard deduction. Most taxpayers take the standard deduction.
